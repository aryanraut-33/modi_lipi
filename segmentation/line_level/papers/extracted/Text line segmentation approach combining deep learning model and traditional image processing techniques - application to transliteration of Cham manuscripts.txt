Abstract
Text line segmentation in historical document images is a crucial task in document analy-
sis and understanding. Historical documents often feature degraded and complex layouts,
and they may exhibit distinct writing styles and languages, posing challenges for text line
segmentation. Our study focuses on the text line segmentation task for Cham manuscript
images. Cham manuscripts, written on Chinese paper and composed in Middle Cham, serve
as precious sources to understand the daily lives of the inhabitants of the Champa King-
dom and provide insights into Champa possessions and their management. To address the
challenges of text line segmentation in Cham manuscript images, this study proposes an
approach that combines deep learning methods with traditional image processing techniques.
Our approach takes into account the characteristics of Cham manuscripts to effectively tackle
the challenges posed by these documents. Speciﬁcally, the procedure begins by leveraging
pre-trained deep learning models and includes a ﬁne-tuning step to extract the candidate
baseline. Then, various image post-processing algorithms are introduced to further improve
segmentation accuracy. Extensive experiments on our Cham manuscripts dataset consisting
of 627 manuscripts with approximately 8300 segmented text lines using different evaluation
criteria demonstrate the signiﬁcant superiority of our approach over existing methods. Our
method outperforms state-of-the-art performance by a considerable margin.

1 Introduction
Historical documents contain many invaluable information which reﬂects the events of the
past. Each document was produced using different material, writing format, and language
leading to different methods for exploring these documents. Document analysis has been
studied for long periods and has achieved promising results on printed documents.
However, when it comes to handwritten documents like Balinese manuscripts [1] or Khmer
manuscripts [2], as well as Cham manuscripts [3], which exhibit distinct writing styles and
languages, the overall performance of existing methods drops signiﬁcantly [1]. Therefore,
further adaptation of the baseline approaches is usually necessary to achieve satisfactory
results in these cases.
Analyzing a document generally involves two main steps: analysis and recognition. During
analysis, the structure of the document, including pages, paragraphs, text lines, and words, is
identiﬁed [4]. Recognition poses a challenge to researchers because the quality of preceding
steps inﬂuences the accuracy of this step, particularly the segmentation of text lines. In the
case of the Cham language studied in this work, we have a transliteration task (converting
text from one script to another according to speciﬁc rules) which depends heavily on the
accurate recognition of descenders, ascenders, and diacritics. In general, transliteration can
be categorized into two main types: text-to-text, which converts text directly from one script
to another, and image-to-text, which is similar to Optical Character Recognition (OCR)
systems. For our task, the objective aligns with image-to-text transliteration, where we aim
to convert images of text into a different script. Therefore, accurate analysis of text-lines is
essential in the whole process.
This study focuses on text line analysis of Cham manuscripts. Since the demise of Champa
kingdoms during the 19th century, the Cham language that originated and developed from 2nd
century, is no longer used among the descendants of the Champa. The lack of transmission
of knowledge and documents of the Cham culture makes the study of this language difﬁcult
for epigraphists and historians.
Considering the Cham language and the history of the Champa kingdom (which spanned
from the 2nd to the 19th centuries and was located in the central region of present-day
Vietnam), historical documents can be classiﬁed into two main types: inscriptions and
manuscripts. The inscriptions, mainly in the Old Cham (or Sanskrit) language, are engraved
onstonesurfaces.Theymainlydocumentreligiousgifts,andpraisestodeities,kings,andtheir
families. Cham inscription images are obtained through a stamping and scanning process.
On the other hand, the manuscripts, written on “Chinese” paper, are composed in Middle
Cham and have also been digitized. These documents shed light on the daily lives of the
inhabitants of the Champa Kingdom and provide insight into the Champa possessions and
their management.
In previous work [5], we studied the segmentation of text-lines for Cham inscriptions.
However, as the complexity of lines of text increases (manifested by skewed text, vari-
able layouts, short lines, or abundant diacritics), the effectiveness of approaches based on
histogram projection proﬁle decreases on Cham manuscripts. Such methods struggle to accu-
rately detect certain features, leading to errors such as missing short text lines, incorrect line
lengths, and inaccurate identiﬁcation of candidate text-lines as shown in Fig. 1.

2 Related works
A text-line refers to a group of characters, symbols, and words that are contiguous and
spatially proximate, allowing for a straight line to be drawn through them [4]. Text-line
segmentation methods can generally be categorized into three main approaches: top-down,
bottom-up, and deep learning-based. For a comprehensive survey of this task, particularly
concerning historical documents, interested readers can refer to [10].
Top-down approaches The top-down approach, pioneered by works such as the projection
proﬁles method [11], relies on analyzing projection proﬁles that sum the number of pixels
projected horizontally or vertically (depending on the direction under study). Peaks in the
proﬁle represent text segments (text lines), while valleys indicate spaces between lines (and

39147
vice versa if the text is black and the background is white). To mitigate local maxima,
the projection proﬁle is often smoothed using techniques like a Gaussian ﬁlter [12] or a
moving average ﬁlter [13]. To address skewed and curved lines, partial projection proﬁles
are employed [14–17]. In this method, the document is vertically divided into sub-columns (or
rows, depending on the layout). Each sub-column’s local maxima in the projection proﬁle
are considered candidate lines. These candidates are then matched using predeﬁned rules
[17] or by measuring distances between them [14]. However, challenges arise when dealing
with documents containing numerous lines of varying lengths or spacing between them. The
projection proﬁle values may not accurately identify candidate lines in such cases. In [18],
based on the assumption of writing in the ﬁlled document (writing from left side to right), the
partial projection proﬁle (of ﬁrst 100 to 200 columns) is used ﬁrst to estimate the number of
text lines and its candidate points. Text-line is created by computing the histogram in three
directions: straight, upward, or downward.
Another top-down approach is the seam carving-based method [14, 19–21]. This tech-
nique uses seam carving to segment text-lines, aiming to minimize the accumulated energy
map computed from the image. High energy values indicate text areas, while low energy
values represent background areas. In [19], a two-step seam carving process is proposed.
First, seam seeds, mainly representing the base text line, are generated from the seam-map
generation in two passes. Then, separating lines are extracted by determining the lower and
upper boundaries for each seam seed. Similarly, in [14] they proposed a two-step strategy
using partial projection proﬁles. In [21], the Labeling, Cutting, Grouping (LCG) method is
introduced, which relies on a new energy map and seam casting for text line segmentation.
A related method is the A* path planning approach [22], which ﬁnds separation lines by
minimizing different cost functions to traverse from the starting state (s) to the goal state (g).
Bottom-up approaches In the case of bottom-up approaches, various techniques have been
explored. For instance, in [23–26] different topological features such as blocks, connected
components, search trees, and heuristic rules are used to extract text lines. One of the earliest
methods, known as smearing, has been extensively employed in the literature [27–30]. The
pioneering work in this domain was presented in [27], where a sequence of pixels is smeared
horizontally (or vertically, depending on the document structure) over a deﬁned distance.
The connected components identiﬁed on the smeared image serve as the boundaries of text-
lines. Traditional smearing methods typically operate on binary input documents. In [30],
the authors introduce an Adaptive Local Contrast Maximization (ALCM) technique, which
transforms the grayscale input image into a new map space where higher values correspond
to text pixels. To address challenges posed by variable documents (such as shorter lines or
irregular line spacing), the ALCM method is extended by incorporating different ﬁlters, such
as the steerable ﬁlter [29] and the isotropic Gaussian ﬁlter [24]. In [24], instead of relying
on a single ﬁlter, the smearing image L is generated through a combination of smoothed
versions of the input image. Subsequently, a component tree is used for the binarization
of the smearing image. Validated connected components are then identiﬁed as text-lines. To
address issues such as labeling disconnected components or removing spurious lines, various
functions are applied in a post-processing step. The ﬁnal text-lines are derived by minimizing
a deﬁned objective function. In [26], based on the heuristics rules of the font size of printed
documents, the connected components are aligned to create a text line. This method proves
quite good results on some speciﬁc font types, but the robustness is not guaranteed. In general,
a drawback of this approach arises when the structure of the text-line is fragmented, as the
smearing process may fail to merge the components seamlessly. Additionally, if the space

Deep learning based approaches Recently, there has been a sharp increase in the devel-
opment of deep learning-based methods for text line segmentation [6, 31–37]. Drawing
inspiration from the semantic segmentation and objection detection tasks in computer vision,
various kind convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been explored for addressing the
text line segmentation problem [6, 31, 35, 36].
For the semantic segmentation, in [31], the authors adapted the fully convolutional
networks (FCNs) [38] to address text line segmentation, leveraging an encoder-decoder
architecture. Similarly, [36] used FCNs but with notable distinctions from [31]: there is
no resolution change in the input image throughout the encoder and decoder, and dilated
convolutions are employed instead of transpose convolutions or upsampling. Dilated con-
volutions are favored because detecting lines of text does not require extensive context for
accurate segmentation. In [34], the original Unet architecture [39] was adapted for text line
segmentation.
Considering the objection detection, in [37], the authors proposed a new model by mod-
iﬁcation the original faster R-CNN by using the Resnet50 and different scaling factors of
the anchor boxes. The proposed architecture has a problem when the text line is skewed, the
bounding box may touch both the previous and the next text-line.
Typically, results from deep learning models manifest themselves as disconnected or
noisy components, where a single line is fragmented into multiple components. To reﬁne
the segmentation, post-processing steps incorporating heuristic cost functions [31] or line
adjacency graphs [35] are used. In [33], a similar approach of integrating deep learning
with image processing was adopted. Initially, a deep convolution network named ARU-
Net is employed to classify each pixel into distinct classes such as baseline, separator, and
others. Subsequently, superpixels are clustered and merged into text lines based on certain ad
hoc information. In contrast, [32] adopted an unsupervised approach by training a Siamese
network to discern the similarity between pairs of patches extracted randomly from a list of
cropped patches of the original image. The trained model serves as an embedding network
to extract image blobs, which are then segmented into appropriate text lines using energy
minimization combining various cost functions.

3 An advanced approach for text line segmentation
In this study, we focus on Cham manuscripts, which are characterized by complex scripts
featuring ascenders, descenders, and diacritics critical for accurate character transliteration.
These complex script elements require careful consideration during text-line segmentation.

Notably, the layout of these manuscripts closely resembles well-studied datasets such as

Our proposed method consists of two main steps: baseline extraction step and segmenta-
tion reﬁnement step with seam carving and minimum spanning tree (MST). In detail, the ﬁrst
step involves using a ﬁne-tuned docExtractor model to identify and extract text line baselines
from the input manuscripts (blue block). Following baseline extraction, two post-processing
algorithms (merging and splitting) further reﬁne the extracted baselines. A customized seam
carving method is then employed to enhance text line separation. Finally, additional reﬁne-
ment is made to the text lines generated through MST to ensure precise boundary delineation
improving the accuracy of line separation. A pre-trained SAE [44] model is used to generate
a binarized image of the manuscript, which serves as input for the minimum spanning tree
(MST) algorithm. The proposed pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 4.

3.2 Segmentation refinement step
3.2.1 Seam carving
As shown in Fig. 8, the segmentation results from the baseline model fail to accurately cover
thetextlines,withinitialsegmentationoftenmisalignedwiththetextregions.Toaddressthese
limitations, seam carving is applied to dynamically reﬁne the boundaries of the segmented
areas by adjusting pixel content. Subsequently, a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) algorithm
is used to further reﬁne and optimize the segmentation results. The combination of seam
carving with MST ensures more precise delineation of the text-lines, signiﬁcantly enhancing
the overall performance of the baseline extraction model.
Energy map construction To cast seams, an energy map must be constructed to guide the
seams on how to separate the text lines. Several methods can be used to compute the energy
map including Gray-level Distance Transform (GDT) [45], gradient map (GM) [14], or a
combination of background energy and text energy map (LCG) as proposed in [21].

the connected components. Therefore, any errors in binarization may propagate through the
graph construction process, adversely affecting the delineation of the resulting text areas.

This section introduces the Cham manuscript dataset used in this work, comprising nearly
11,000 pages of Cham handwritten manuscripts written on “Chinese” paper. Figure 12
demonstrates some examples of Cham manuscripts in the dataset. The corpus encompasses
manuscripts in two languages and scripts: Han Nom (about 5% of the corpus) and Middle
Cham. To be more precise, we focused solely on the distribution of data related to the
Cham language, excluding the Han Nom language from our analysis. A large part of the
manuscripts can be dated (from Vietnamese seals) between 1702 and 1810, making this set
of manuscripts a coherent corpus of 18th century texts. These manuscripts are the proof of
Champa’s possessions and their management. These documents relate the daily life of the
inhabitants of ancient Panduranga during more than one century and complete our perception
of the complex process, also little known, of the annexation of the Panduranga by the Nguyen
dynasty. The alphabet used was originally borrowed from India to write inscriptions in San-

In this work, rather than directly evaluating the performance of text line segmentation,

task. To accomplish this, we utilize the state-of-the-art Two Step Sequence Transformer

[48]. For the transliteration aspect, we use Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error

model performance by computing the Levenshtein distance between the ground truth and the

• Ic, Iw: the number of inserted characters/ words.

text line segmentation results of different models (Table 6).

improvement in models like docExtractor [7], eScriptorum [9] and LayoutParser [40] when

6 Conclusions and future works
In this paper, we propose an efﬁcient method to address the challenge of segmenting text
lines in Cham manuscripts, leveraging both pre-trained deep learning models and traditional
image processing techniques. Through comprehensive experiments conducted on our Cham
manuscript dataset and analysis of various metrics encompassing both segmentation and
transliteration, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach compared with the state-of-
the-art methods. Experimental results also conﬁrm the role of the text line segmentation step
in the whole process of document analysis. In the future, we aim to enhance the splitting algo-
rithm to facilitate text line segmentation without sacriﬁcing crucial information. Additionally,
given the disparity between manual and model-based segmentation results, ﬁne-tuning the
transliteration model becomes imperative, especially considering that the current model is
trained solely on manual segmentation. Finally, our objective is to establish a comprehensive
end-to-end framework for the comprehensive understanding of Cham manuscripts.
